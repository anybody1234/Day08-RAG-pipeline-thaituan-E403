## Topic 1: Education and Learning

**Q: How has the way people learn changed in recent years?**
The transformation has been quite remarkable, really. The traditional model of learning—someone lecturing at the front of a classroom while students take notes—has been significantly disrupted by technology. Online platforms have democratised access to knowledge in ways that weren't possible even a decade ago. Someone in a remote village can now access lectures from world-class universities.

But I think the more profound shift is in how we conceptualise learning itself. There's a growing recognition that learning should be more active and experiential rather than passive absorption of facts. The rise of project-based learning, MOOCs, and micro-credentials reflects this change. We're moving away from the idea of education as something that happens once in your youth towards a model of continuous, lifelong learning.

That said, I think there's value in not dismissing traditional methods entirely. There's still something irreplaceable about face-to-face interaction with a skilled teacher who can respond to your specific confusion in real time.

**Q: What role should technology play in education?**
Technology should complement rather than replace traditional teaching methods. The best uses of technology in education are those that enhance what's already working—personalised learning paths, instant feedback on assessments, access to resources and simulations that wouldn't otherwise be available.

However, we need to be cautious about assuming technology automatically improves outcomes. There's a lot of hype around ed-tech that isn't always backed by evidence. And we shouldn't overlook the social dimension of education—children learn important social skills in classrooms that can't be replicated online.

I'm particularly interested in how AI might personalise learning in the future. Imagine software that can identify exactly where a student is struggling and adapt the curriculum in real time. That's genuinely exciting, though we need to think carefully about the implications.

**Q: Should universities be free for all students?**
This is a question that sparks considerable debate. On one hand, free university education would increase social mobility and ensure that talented students from less affluent backgrounds aren't excluded from higher education. Countries like Germany have shown it's financially feasible.

On the other hand, introducing or maintaining fees can actually benefit students in some ways—it creates accountability and forces institutions to compete on quality. There's also the question of whether taxpayer funding should go to university education when many other areas, like primary and secondary schooling, might need resources more urgently.

I think the ideal approach probably involves some combination: means-tested support to ensure access isn't purely determined by wealth, alongside quality assurance mechanisms to ensure universities don't become diploma mills. The specifics would depend on each country's economic context and existing educational infrastructure.

## Topic 2: Society and Social Media

**Q: How has social media changed the way people communicate?**
It's transformed communication in ways we're still grappling with. On the positive side, it's made communication faster, cheaper, and more accessible. I can maintain relationships with friends scattered across the globe with minimal effort. For many people, especially those who struggle with face-to-face interaction, social media has been genuinely liberating.

But the drawbacks are becoming increasingly apparent. The curated nature of most social media creates unrealistic expectations and fosters comparison anxiety. The brevity encouraged by platforms like Twitter has arguably dumbed down public discourse. And the algorithmic nature of these platforms means we increasingly live in information bubbles, exposed primarily to views we already agree with.

There's also the paradox that while we're more connected than ever in terms of quantity of contact, the quality of our interactions may have suffered. Many people report feeling lonelier despite having hundreds of online friends. The depth of conversation has been replaced by surface-level engagement through likes and comments.

**Q: Do you think social media has a negative impact on mental health?**
The evidence suggests that, for certain people and certain types of usage, there is a correlation with poorer mental health outcomes—particularly among adolescents, and particularly for heavy users of image-focused platforms like Instagram.

However, I think it's important not to oversimplify. The relationship is complex and probably varies significantly depending on how social media is used. Passive scrolling through others' highlight reels is likely more harmful than active engagement and genuine connection. And for some people—those with anxiety, for instance—social media can provide a less intimidating way to maintain social contact.

The responsibility probably lies with both platforms and users. Platforms should be more transparent about the psychological effects of their products and implement design changes that mitigate harm. Users, particularly parents of young people, need to be educated about healthy usage patterns.

**Q: Should governments regulate social media?**
Some regulation seems inevitable and probably necessary. We're already seeing legislation emerge around issues like data privacy, the spread of misinformation, and the targeting of children. The EU's approach with the Digital Services Act is an interesting model.

However, regulation must be carefully balanced against free speech concerns. Governments have a poor track record when it comes to using social media regulation to suppress dissent. The best approach probably involves industry standards and self-regulation alongside government oversight, with meaningful penalties for platforms that fail to protect users.

The challenge is that technology evolves much faster than regulatory frameworks can adapt. By the time legislation catches up with one concern, new platforms and practices have emerged that fall outside existing rules. This suggests we need more adaptive, principles-based regulation rather than rigid rules.

## Topic 3: Work and Career

**Q: How has the nature of work changed in recent decades?**
The changes have been quite fundamental. The traditional career model—in which someone would work for a single employer for decades, climbing a clear hierarchical ladder—has given way to something far more fluid. Contract work, freelancing, and portfolio careers have become mainstream for many professions.

Technology has been the primary driver of this change. Remote work has become normalised, particularly following the pandemic. Communication tools like Slack and video conferencing have dissolved geographical constraints. Workers no longer need to be physically present to collaborate effectively.

There's also been a shift in expectations. Job security and pension benefits have given way to expectations of flexibility, purpose, and work-life balance. Employees, particularly younger generations, increasingly prioritise meaningful work over high salaries. This has forced companies to rethink their value propositions to attract talent.

The downside is that this new flexibility often translates to insecurity for workers. Gig economy workers typically lack benefits and protections that traditional employees enjoy. There's a genuine tension between the freedom the new model offers and the precarity it can create.

**Q: What skills will be most important in the future workplace?**
Adaptability is probably the most crucial skill—being able to learn new things quickly and adjust to changing circumstances. Given how rapidly technology evolves, the specific skills in demand today may be obsolete tomorrow. What matters is the ability to acquire new skills as needed.

Creativity and complex problem-solving will also remain highly valued, as these are areas where human capabilities are harder to replicate than routine cognitive or manual tasks. Similarly, emotional intelligence—understanding and relating to others effectively—will become more rather than less important as routine work is automated.

Communication skills, particularly the ability to collaborate across cultural and linguistic boundaries, will be essential. As work becomes more distributed and global, the ability to build rapport and maintain effective relationships without face-to-face contact becomes crucial.

**Q: Do you think robots will replace human workers?**
In certain sectors, yes, we've already seen significant automation. Manufacturing, logistics, and increasingly certain aspects of white-collar work like data analysis and even some legal and medical diagnostics are being transformed by AI and robotics.

However, I don't think human obsolescence is imminent. Many tasks—those requiring genuine creativity, nuanced judgement, complex social interaction, or physical dexterity in unpredictable environments—remain beyond the reach of current technology. And there's the economic consideration: if automation becomes so widespread that consumers have no income to spend, the economic rationale for further automation collapses.

The more likely scenario is transformation rather than wholesale replacement. Certain roles will disappear, new ones will emerge, and most jobs will involve working alongside increasingly sophisticated tools. The challenge for society will be managing this transition equitably—ensuring that the benefits of automation are widely shared rather than concentrated among those who own the technology.
