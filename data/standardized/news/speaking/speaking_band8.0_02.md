## Model Answer 1: A Person Who Inspired You

**Describe someone who has inspired you to achieve your goals.**

I'd like to talk about my university professor, Dr. Sarah Chen, who profoundly influenced both my career path and my approach to learning. I first encountered her during a seminar on cognitive psychology in my second year, and what immediately struck me was her genuine passion for the subject—she neverlectured from slides, but instead led discussions as if we were fellow researchers exploring ideas together.

The reason she's been such an inspiration stems from a specific incident. After receiving a mediocre grade on an essay, I was ready to accept it as just one of those setbacks. But Dr. Chen invited me to her office and spent nearly an hour going through my work, not to justify her marking, but to help me understand where my argument could have been stronger. She saw potential in me that I hadn't yet recognised in myself.

What made her different from other academics was her accessibility and the way she treated every student's ideas with genuine respect, regardless of whether they were top performers or struggling. She kept her office door open, literally, and made it clear that questions—even ones that might seem obvious—were always welcome.

Her influence on me has been lasting. She encouraged me to pursue postgraduate research, which I initially thought was beyond my capabilities. Without her belief in me, I wouldn't be working in academia today. More than that, I've adopted her teaching philosophy in my own work: that the best learning happens when students feel genuinely heard and challenged in equal measure.

## Model Answer 2: A Historical Period

**Describe a period of history that you would like to learn more about.**

I'd love to explore the Renaissance period more deeply, particularly the 15th and 16th centuries in Italy. There's something fascinating about an era when art, science, and philosophy seemed to evolve almost simultaneously, with each discipline feeding into the others in unexpected ways.

What draws me to this period is the remarkable spirit of inquiry that defined it. Figures like Leonardo da Vinci embodied the ideal of the polymath—someone equally at home dissecting a corpse to understand human anatomy as painting the Mona Lisa or sketching designs for flying machines. I find that multidisciplinary approach incredibly inspiring in an age when we're increasingly encouraged to specialise.

I'm particularly interested in how the printing press transformed communication and the spread of ideas during this period. Gutenberg's invention around 1440 fundamentally changed what was possible in terms of knowledge sharing, much like the internet has done in our own time. The parallel feels significant.

I think understanding this period better would give me insight into how creative and intellectual movements gain momentum. The Renaissance didn't happen in isolation—it was driven by patronage, trade routes that brought diverse ideas together, and a relative period of prosperity that allowed people to invest in art and learning. These conditions feel relevant when considering how we might foster innovation today.

If I had the opportunity, I'd love to visit Florence and walk the same streets that Michelangelo and Machiavelli once did, standing before works of art that redefined what was possible in human expression.

## Model Answer 3: An Important Skill

**Describe an important skill that you learned as an adult.**

One skill I've developed relatively recently is public speaking, which I started working on about three years ago when I joined a local toastmasters club. Looking back, I find it somewhat embarrassing that I avoided it for so long, given how much it's enriched both my professional and personal life.

I first decided to address this after a particularly mortifying experience at work—I was asked to present a project update to the board with virtually no notice, and I completely froze. My hands trembled, my voice wavered, and I rushed through the entire presentation in what felt like half the time it should have taken. The feedback was gentle but clear: I needed to work on my delivery.

Learning to speak publicly was genuinely challenging. It wasn't about learning the content—I knew my material well. The difficulty was retraining my body's response to standing in front of a group. The toastmasters programme helped enormously through its structured approach: starting with very short prepared speeches and gradually building up to longer, more spontaneous talks. The supportive environment made a crucial difference—there was no judgment, only feedback aimed at helping everyone improve.

The skill has proved invaluable in ways I hadn't anticipated. Obviously it's helped at work, where I now regularly present to clients and stakeholders without anxiety. But unexpectedly, it's also improved my everyday conversations. I've become more conscious of how I structure my thoughts when speaking, making me a clearer communicator overall. It's been a reminder that abilities we assume are fixed can actually be developed with deliberate practice.

## Model Answer 4: A Goal You Achieved

**Describe a significant goal you achieved that took a long time to accomplish.**

The goal I'd like to talk about is learning to speak conversational Spanish, which I finally achieved after about four years of on-and-off study. This might sound modest compared to some achievements, but for me it represented a significant personal victory.

My motivation was partly practical—I travel frequently for work to South America—and partly personal. My grandmother was originally from Colombia, and I grew up feeling a connection to the culture that I couldn't fully embrace because of the language barrier. As I got older, this felt like a gap in my identity that I wanted to address.

The process was far from linear. I tried various approaches: apps, online courses, textbooks, even a few months of formal evening classes. Each had its merits, but none was sufficient on its own. The breakthrough came when I started having regular conversation exchanges with a native speaker from Madrid via video call. Being forced to think on my feet, even when I made embarrassing mistakes, accelerated my progress in ways that passive study never could.

The moment I realised I'd achieved my goal wasn't dramatic. It was during a business trip to Buenos Aires, when I found myself ordering dinner, making small talk with hotel staff, and even joking with a taxi driver—all without reaching for my phone to translate. I hadn't become fluent, but I could function comfortably in everyday situations, which was exactly what I'd hoped for.

Looking back, the main lesson I took from this experience is that consistent, daily practice—even just fifteen minutes—is far more effective than sporadic intensive sessions. Language learning, like many skills, requires sustained exposure rather than cramming.
